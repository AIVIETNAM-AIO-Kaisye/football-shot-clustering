\section{Abstract}

Clustering football player styles is a fundamental challenge in modern sports analytics with immediate applications in tactical profiling, opponent preparation, and recruitment scouting. While the K-Means algorithm is widely adopted for partition-based clustering, its practical performance depends heavily on feature scaling transformations, the choice of the cluster hyperparameter $k$, and rigorous validation protocols. In football event streams, performance metrics often follow heavily right-skewed distributions driven by elite outliers (e.g., top-tier goalscorers and high-volume playmakers), making traditional normalization techniques vulnerable to distortion.

This study presents an end-to-end, data-driven methodology for unsupervised player style clustering using K-Means. We analyze 1,016 qualified players ($\ge 900$ minutes played) across 1,140 matches from three premier European competitions (Premier League, La Liga, Serie A) during the complete 2015/2016 season using open StatsBomb event data. Each player is represented across 29 per-90 rate and efficiency metrics spanning shooting, passing, dribbling, defensive engagement, and spatial pitch coordinates.

Our empirical findings demonstrate three core conclusions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Robust Scaling is Essential:} RobustScaler substantially outperforms Unscaled and StandardScaler representations by utilizing order statistics (median and interquartile range), effectively mitigating the distortion of extreme outliers and constructing a resilient, scale-invariant feature space.
    \item \textbf{Optimal Cluster Resolution ($k=5$):} While mathematical criteria such as the Silhouette score globally peak at $k=2$ (a coarse binary split into defensive and offensive units), selecting $k=5$ achieves the optimal balance between mathematical cohesion (forming a strong local elbow and peak) and domain interpretability across specialized tactical roles.
    \item \textbf{High Stability and Boundary Learnability:} The five discovered clusters demonstrate strong structural alignment with real-world playing positions (Overall Purity $\approx 69.7\%$, $\text{NMI} \approx 0.546$). Furthermore, a supervised K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classifier achieves $96.6\% \pm 0.8\%$ 5-fold cross-validation accuracy, confirming that the unsupervised partitions form highly distinct, stable decision boundaries in 29-dimensional space.
    \item \textbf{Fine-Grained Sub-Clustering (Case Study):} As an illustrative expansion, we apply a secondary K-Means pass on the 152-player striker cluster to reveal three distinct tactical archetypes within this positional group: Poacher, Target Man, and Deep-Lying Forward. This demonstrates the natural extensibility of the pipeline to hierarchical fine-grained role discovery.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Introduction}

The rapid evolution of modern sports analytics has transformed how football clubs, scouts, and performance analysts evaluate player performance. Historically, player evaluation relied on subjective visual scouting combined with rudimentary aggregate statistics such as total goals, assists, or clean sheets. However, modern football is defined by tactical fluidity, high-tempo pressing systems, and positional interchangeability (such as inverted fullbacks, false nines, and roaming playmakers). Consequently, nominal lineup labels (e.g., "Midfielder" or "Defender") fail to capture the nuanced on-pitch behavioral roles executed by players over the course of a competitive season.

With the advent of fine-grained event data streams provided by organizations such as StatsBomb \cite{statsbomb_open_data}, every on-ball action—including passes, shots, pressures, recoveries, and spatial pitch coordinates—is recorded with micro-level precision. This granular data allows analysts to construct comprehensive behavioral profiles that reflect what players actually do on the pitch rather than what their designated position implies. Unsupervised machine learning, specifically partition-based clustering, offers a systematic framework to discover natural tactical archetypes directly from these multidimensional event distributions.

Despite its popularity, the application of K-Means clustering \cite{macqueen1967methods} to football event data presents significant methodological challenges:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Scale Disparity and Heavy-Tailed Skewness:} Event metrics vary dramatically in scale. For instance, a central midfielder may record over 100 touches per match, whereas a striker may record only 2 to 3 shots per match. Furthermore, exceptional individual performances generate heavy-tailed, right-skewed distributions. Standard distance metrics like Euclidean distance are easily dominated by high-variance features unless appropriate scaling is applied.
  \item \textbf{Hyperparameter Selection Ambiguity:} Determining the appropriate number of clusters ($k$) requires balancing mathematical validation metrics against domain interpretability. Pure mathematical criteria often favor overly coarse binary splits (such as dividing players broadly into defensive versus offensive units), necessitating a multi-criteria model selection framework.
  \item \textbf{Cluster Validation and Stability:} Unsupervised clusters risk being mathematical artifacts of centroid initialization unless verified through external domain benchmarks and boundary stability testing.
\end{itemize}

This study addresses these challenges through a rigorous empirical investigation using complete 2015/2016 season data from three premier European leagues. Importantly, ground-truth player positions (\texttt{primary\_position}) are held out strictly as hidden evaluation targets and are never exposed during feature engineering or cluster fitting.

\textbf{Research Questions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1 (Feature Scaling Impact):} How do different scaling strategies (Unscaled, StandardScaler, RobustScaler) affect the topological geometry of the feature space and the resulting K-Means cluster partitions?
  \item \textbf{RQ2 (Optimal Cluster Resolution):} Which cluster cardinality $k \in [2, 10]$ maximizes internal cohesion and inter-cluster separation across the Elbow (WCSS), Silhouette Score, and Gap Statistic metrics?
  \item \textbf{RQ3 (Tactical Alignment and Stability):} To what extent do the discovered unsupervised clusters correspond to ground-truth playing positions, and how robust are these cluster boundaries under cross-validated supervised classification?
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Paper Organization:} The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section~\ref{sec:related_work} reviews related literature on football analytics and clustering methodologies. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} formalizes the mathematical foundations of K-Means, scaling transformations, and evaluation metrics. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data processing pipeline, player inclusion filtering, and feature extraction. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the empirical results, model selection curves, tactical archetype interpretations, and stability tests. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses practical applications for scouting and recruitment, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

Player profiling and tactical clustering in association football have evolved across three methodological domains:

\subsection{Positional vs. Behavioral Profiling}
Early sports statistics categorized players almost exclusively by static nominal roles assigned on team sheets. However, modern tactical paradigms (such as Pep Guardiola's *Juego de Posición* and high-intensity *Gegenpressing*) require players to fulfill multiple functional duties regardless of their starting position. Recent sports analytics literature emphasizes behavioral profiling derived from event data and tracking coordinates \cite{pedregosa2011scikit}. By aggregating event frequencies into per-90 rates and spatial distribution metrics, researchers can identify functional player roles, tactical versatility, and squad redundancy without relying on rigid positional constraints.

\subsection{Distance-Based Clustering in Sports}
The K-Means algorithm \cite{macqueen1967methods} remains the benchmark unsupervised learning technique in sports analytics due to its computational scalability and intuitive centroid-based cluster representations. However, distance-based algorithms that rely on Euclidean geometry are notoriously sensitive to feature scaling and the presence of outliers. Standard normalization methods, such as $z$-score standardization, rely on the empirical mean and standard deviation, which are easily skewed by heavy-tailed performance metrics \cite{hubert1985comparing}. While robust scaling based on order statistics (medians and interquartile ranges) is widely recognized in robust statistics, comprehensive empirical comparisons of scaling resilience in multi-league football event data remain limited.

\subsection{Cluster Validation and Stability}
A persistent limitation in applied unsupervised learning is the lack of formal validation. Unsupervised algorithms will partition any given dataset into $k$ groups, regardless of whether true cluster structure exists. Robust validation frameworks require two complementary perspectives: (1) internal validation metrics, such as the Silhouette index \cite{rousseeuw1987silhouettes} and the Gap Statistic \cite{tibshirani2001estimating}, which evaluate cluster separation against null reference distributions; and (2) external validation and stability testing, where cluster labels are tested against ground-truth domain categories and verified for boundary learnability using supervised classifiers such as K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN).

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

This section outlines the mathematical formulations and theoretical foundations of the machine learning algorithms, normalization procedures, and validation metrics used throughout this study.

\subsection{K-Means Formulation}
Let $X = \{\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_n\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ denote the dataset of $n = 1016$ players, where each player vector $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{29}$ represents 29 normalized performance features. The objective of K-Means is to partition the $n$ observations into $k$ disjoint clusters $C = \{C_1, C_2, \ldots, C_k\}$ such that the Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS) is minimized:

\begin{equation}
\text{WCSS}(C) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in C_j} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j\|^2
\end{equation}

where $\boldsymbol{\mu}_j$ is the centroid vector representing the arithmetic mean of cluster $C_j$:

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\mu}_j = \frac{1}{|C_j|} \sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in C_j} \mathbf{x}_i
\end{equation}

and $\|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j\|$ represents the standard $L_2$ Euclidean distance metric:

\begin{equation}
\|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j\| = \sqrt{\sum_{l=1}^{d} (x_{il} - \mu_{jl})^2}
\end{equation}

The optimization is solved iteratively using Lloyd's algorithm: (1) in the assignment step, each sample is assigned to its nearest centroid; (2) in the update step, cluster centroids are recomputed based on newly assigned members. Convergence is achieved when centroid positions stabilize within a tolerance threshold $\epsilon = 10^{-4}$.

\subsection{Feature Scaling}
Because the Euclidean distance computation is non-invariant to linear feature scaling, variables with large absolute ranges dominate the objective function. We evaluate three distinct scaling transformations $T(\mathbf{x})$:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Unscaled ($X_{\text{unscaled}}$):} Raw feature values without transformation:
  \begin{equation}
  T(x_{ij}) = x_{ij}
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{StandardScaler ($X_{\text{standard}}$):} Centers features around the sample mean $\mu_j$ and scales by the standard deviation $\sigma_j$:
  \begin{equation}
  T(x_{ij}) = \frac{x_{ij} - \mu_j}{\sigma_j}, \quad \text{where } \mu_j = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{ij}, \quad \sigma_j = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_{ij} - \mu_j)^2}
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{RobustScaler ($X_{\text{robust}}$):} Centers features using the sample median and scales by the Interquartile Range (IQR):
  \begin{equation}
  T(x_{ij}) = \frac{x_{ij} - \text{Median}(x_j)}{\text{IQR}(x_j)} = \frac{x_{ij} - \text{Q2}(x_j)}{\text{Q3}(x_j) - \text{Q1}(x_j)}
  \end{equation}
  where $\text{Q1}(x_j)$, $\text{Q2}(x_j)$, and $\text{Q3}(x_j)$ represent the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of feature $j$, respectively. By utilizing rank-based order statistics rather than parametric moments, RobustScaler prevents extreme statistical outliers from compressing the dynamic range of typical observations.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Internal Model Selection}

\subsubsection{Elbow Method (WCSS)}
The Elbow method evaluates the rate of reduction in WCSS as $k$ increases. The optimal $k^*$ corresponds to the inflection point ("elbow") beyond which adding further clusters yields diminishing marginal reductions in intra-cluster dispersion.

\subsubsection{Silhouette Coefficient}
For an observation $\mathbf{x}_i \in C_j$, let $a(i)$ denote the mean intra-cluster distance to all other points within the same cluster $C_j$:
\begin{equation}
a(i) = \frac{1}{|C_j| - 1} \sum_{\mathbf{x}_m \in C_j, m \neq i} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_m\|
\end{equation}

Let $b(i)$ denote the minimum mean distance from $\mathbf{x}_i$ to points in any neighboring cluster $C_l \neq C_j$:
\begin{equation}
b(i) = \min_{l \neq j} \frac{1}{|C_l|} \sum_{\mathbf{x}_m \in C_l} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_m\|
\end{equation}

The individual Silhouette coefficient $s(i)$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
s(i) = \frac{b(i) - a(i)}{\max(a(i), b(i))}, \quad s(i) \in [-1, 1]
\end{equation}
The global Silhouette Score $S(k) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} s(i)$ reflects overall partition quality, where values closer to $+1$ indicate tight, well-separated clusters.

\subsubsection{Gap Statistic}
The Gap Statistic \cite{tibshirani2001estimating} formalizes cluster validation by comparing the observed log within-cluster dispersion $\log(\text{WCSS}(k))$ against its expected value under an appropriate null reference distribution:
\begin{equation}
\text{Gap}(k) = E_n^*\{\log(\text{WCSS}(k))\} - \log(\text{WCSS}(k))
\end{equation}
where the expected value $E_n^*\{\cdot\}$ is estimated by drawing $B = 10$ Monte Carlo reference datasets from a uniform distribution generated across the hyper-rectangle aligned with the principal components of the empirical dataset.

\subsection{Partition Agreement (ARI)}
To measure partition similarity between two distinct clustering assignments $U = \{u_1, \ldots, u_R\}$ and $V = \{v_1, \ldots, v_C\}$, the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) \cite{hubert1985comparing} evaluates the proportion of sample pairs placed in the same or different clusters, corrected for random chance:
\begin{equation}
\text{ARI} = \frac{\sum_{i,j} \binom{n_{ij}}{2} - \left[\sum_i \binom{a_i}{2} \sum_j \binom{b_j}{2}\right] / \binom{n}{2}}{\frac{1}{2}\left[\sum_i \binom{a_i}{2} + \sum_j \binom{b_j}{2}\right] - \left[\sum_i \binom{a_i}{2} \sum_j \binom{b_j}{2}\right] / \binom{n}{2}}
\end{equation}
where $n_{ij} = |u_i \cap v_j|$, $a_i = \sum_j n_{ij}$, and $b_j = \sum_i n_{ij}$. ARI ranges from $-1$ to $1$, where $1$ indicates identical partitions and values $\le 0$ indicate chance-level agreement.

\subsection{External Validation Metrics}

\subsubsection{Overall Cluster Purity}
Purity measures the extent to which each unsupervised cluster is dominated by samples belonging to a single ground-truth positional category $Y = \{Y_1, \ldots, Y_L\}$:
\begin{equation}
\text{Purity}(C, Y) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^{k} \max_{l \in \{1, \ldots, L\}} |C_j \cap Y_l|
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Normalized Mutual Information (NMI)}
NMI measures the mutual information shared between the unsupervised cluster assignments $C$ and the true positional labels $Y$, normalized by their respective Shannon entropies:
\begin{equation}
\text{NMI}(C, Y) = \frac{2 \cdot I(C; Y)}{H(C) + H(Y)}
\end{equation}
where $I(C; Y) = \sum_{j,l} \frac{|C_j \cap Y_l|}{n} \log \left(\frac{n |C_j \cap Y_l|}{|C_j| |Y_l|}\right)$, and $H(\cdot)$ denotes Shannon entropy.

\subsection{Cluster Stability via KNN}
To verify that the discovered cluster boundaries represent learnable, continuous regions in the 29-dimensional feature space, we employ a supervised K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classifier. Given an unlabelled vector $\mathbf{x}_i$, KNN assigns a cluster prediction based on majority voting among its $n\_neighbors$ nearest Euclidean neighbors:
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_i = \argmax_{c \in \{0, \ldots, k-1\}} \sum_{m \in N_K(\mathbf{x}_i)} \mathbb{I}(y_m = c)
\end{equation}
where $N_K(\mathbf{x}_i)$ represents the index set of the $K$ closest points and $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the binary indicator function. Model accuracy and variance are evaluated using 5-fold Stratified Cross-Validation.

\subsection{Missing Value Imputation}
Four conditional features contained missing values ($NaN$) due to structural non-occurrence: \texttt{avg\_shot\_distance} (75 missing), \texttt{interception\_success\_rate} (72 missing), \texttt{tackle\_success\_rate} (70 missing), and \texttt{dribble\_success\_rate} (42 missing). These instances correspond to players who recorded zero attempts in that specific event category over the entire season (e.g., goalkeepers attempting zero dribbles or shots).

Imputing zero would create severe analytical errors; for example, setting a missing tackle success rate to 0.0 would falsely categorize a player as having attempted tackles and failed 100\% of the time. Instead, we applied median imputation. Because the volume rate for these actions is already captured by corresponding rate metrics (e.g., \texttt{tackles\_p90} $= 0.0$), replacing missing efficiency rates with the median assigns a neutral baseline that prevents artificial distortion in Euclidean distance calculations.

\subsection{Case Study: Fine-Grained Tactical Sub-Clustering of Primary Forwards}
\label{sec:striker_subclustering}

The five macro-clusters provide a high-level tactical map of player behavioral roles across the entire pitch. As an illustrative case study, we apply a secondary K-Means sub-clustering pass exclusively on \textbf{Cluster 1 (Clinical Finishers \& Primary Forwards, $n = 152$)} to demonstrate that the unsupervised pipeline can be extended hierarchically to discover fine-grained tactical archetypes within a single specialized positional group.

\subsubsection{Feature Re-engineering for the Striker Subspace}

Within this 152-player subgroup, 12 features from the global 29-feature matrix are near-constant or tactically uninformative (e.g., \texttt{clearances\_p90}, \texttt{tackles\_p90}, \texttt{interceptions\_p90}) and are excluded. Conversely, a critical feature absent from the macro pipeline is introduced: \texttt{passes\_p90}. This 17-feature striker-specific matrix is re-scaled using \texttt{RobustScaler} fitted exclusively on the 152 forwards.

\textbf{Methodological note:} \texttt{np\_goals\_p90} and \texttt{assists\_p90} are retained within this subgroup because, among forwards of comparable positions, they carry behavioral signal distinguishing pure penalty-box finishers from link-up creators.

\subsubsection{Hyperparameter Evaluation ($k \in [2, 7]$)}

\subsubsection{Methodological Takeaway}

The progression from $k = 4$ to $k = 3$ demonstrates a critical lesson in sports domain unsupervised learning: \textbf{unconstrained clustering algorithms will readily split on player quality if volume variance exceeds positional variance}. By incorporating domain-informed feature re-engineering (\texttt{passes\_p90}) and deliberately pruning quality-outlier clusters ($k = 4 \rightarrow k = 3$), we achieve an interpretable, noise-resilient taxonomy of forward playing styles.

\section{Discussion and Applications}
\label{sec:discussion}

The empirical findings from this study have immediate, practical applications for professional football analytics and recruitment:

\subsection{Player Scouting Applications}
Traditional scouting frequently struggles when attempting to replace departing players who play unique tactical roles. For example, if a team loses an attacking fullback who functions primarily as an inverted creator, searching solely for nominal "Right Backs" on team sheets will overlook suitable candidates from midfield positions. By mapping candidate players into the 29-dimensional behavioral feature space, clubs can compute Euclidean distances between candidate vectors and the target centroid to identify stylistic clones regardless of nominal positioning.

\subsection{Opponent Tactical Analysis}
By aggregating the cluster assignments of an opponent's starting eleven over recent matches, coaching staffs can construct an objective tactical fingerprint of the opposing squad. Identifying whether an opponent deploys two pure wingers (Cluster 3) versus two hybrid central creators (Cluster 4) informs defensive pressing schemes, transition traps, and spatial defensive block heights.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
While this study demonstrates high stability and interpretability, several limitations should be noted:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Temporal Aggregation:} Season-aggregated metrics provide high statistical confidence but obscure match-by-match tactical adjustments, fatigue, or role changes caused by managerial turnover.
  \item \textbf{Context-Free Event Valuing:} Standard per-90 rates treat all passes or pressures equally, without weighting the game state (e.g., winning vs. chasing a lead) or opposition strength.
\end{enumerate}
Future work should integrate possession-value metrics (e.g., Expected Threat / xT), tracking data for off-ball movement, and multi-season longitudinal models to track player tactical evolution over time.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This research provides a comprehensive, mathematically grounded study on unsupervised player style clustering from football event data. By addressing the critical challenges of feature scaling, model selection, and stability validation on 1,016 qualified players from the 2015/2016 European season, our findings confirm:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Scaling is decisive:} RobustScaler normalization based on medians and interquartile ranges is crucial to prevent extreme performance outliers from distorting distance-based clustering.
  \item \textbf{Five tactical archetypes:} Multi-criteria validation across Elbow, Silhouette, and Gap Statistic curves identifies $k=5$ as the optimal resolution, capturing distinct tactical behaviors (Center-Backs, Strikers, Goalkeepers, Playmakers/Wingers, and Hybrid Controllers).
  \item \textbf{High structural stability:} The discovered clusters align strongly with ground-truth positions ($\text{Purity} \approx 69.7\%$) and establish robust decision boundaries validated by a $96.6\%$ KNN cross-validation accuracy.
  \item \textbf{Extensibility to hierarchical sub-clustering:} A secondary K-Means pass on the 152-player striker cluster successfully uncovers three fine-grained tactical archetypes (Poacher, Target Man, Deep-Lying Forward), demonstrating that the pipeline generalizes naturally to hierarchical role discovery. The critical methodological lesson is that subspace-aware feature re-engineering --- not additional hyperparameter tuning --- unlocks meaningful intra-cluster differentiation.
\end{enumerate}

This framework bridges the gap between raw event data streams and interpretable tactical intelligence, providing sports analysts with a principled foundation for objective player profiling.
